A Practical Guide to Statistics

Chapter 6 Significance

There are two types of significance: statistical significance and clinical significance. Consider a research trial finds a statistically significant difference between participants’ depression scores before and after 12 sessions of therapy which amounts to a 1.0 change on the PHQ-9. Despite this statistically significant chaange, it’s unlikely that those participants would feel that this therapy had a meaningful impact on their lives, despite reaching statistical significance.

Statistical significance: Is this effect real or due to random variation? Clinical significance: Is this effect large enough to matter?

6.1 Statistical significance

Statistics is often done using the frequentist (or classical) approach which is based in null hypothesis statistical testing (NHST). This type of statistics always starts with a null hypothesis (H₀) which usually states that there is no difference between groups, conditions or variables and an alternative hypothesis that an effect or difference exists (Ha). After data are collected, a test statistic is calculated. The specific test statistic depends on the analysis being conducted (e.g., t for t-tests, F for ANOVAs, and χ² for chi-square tests). These statistics represent the size of the observed effect relative to the amount of random variation expected under the null hypothesis. In other words, they indicate how far the observed data deviate from what we would expect if there were truly no effect.

The test statistic is then compared against its sampling distribution, which allows a p-value to be calculated. The p-value represents the probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed, assuming that the null hypothesis is true. Therefore, a smaller p-value indicates that the observed data would be relatively unlikely if there were no real effect. For example, a p-value of 0.03 means that, if the null hypothesis were true, there would be a 3% probability of obtaining results this extreme or more extreme due to random variation.

In 1925, Fisher published Statistical Methods for Research Workers and suggested that results occurring less than 5% of the time under the null hypothesis should be considered noteworthy. Over time, this suggestion became formalised into the convention of using p < .05 as a threshold for rejecting the null hypothesis, although Fisher himself did not intend this to be a rigid rule.

An alternative approach to frequentist/classical statistics is Bayesian statistics. A key limitation of NHST is that it does not allow researchers to directly calculate the probability that a hypothesis is true. Instead, it evaluates how compatible the observed data are with the null hypothesis. Bayesian statistics takes a different approach by estimating the relative evidence for competing hypotheses (such as the null and alternative hypotheses) and allows researchers to update their beliefs about hypotheses in light of new data.

6.2 Clinical significance and effect sizes

Clinical significance tells us whether our finding is large enough to be meaningful in pratice. There are several ways of assessing clinical significance. Three common approaches are:

1. Reporting an effect size: this tells us how large the observed difference or association is, rather than simply whether it is statistically significant.

2. Comparing the effect with a pre-established minimally clinically important difference (MCID): this tells us whether the change is large enough to be considered meaningful from a clinical perspective.

3. Reporting the proportion of participants who achieve a clinically meaningful improvement: for example, the percentage of patients whose symptoms improve by at least a pre-defined amount.

6.2.1 Effect sizes

An effect size is a measure of the magnitude of a finding. The appropriate effect sizes depends on the type of analysis being conducted. For example:

Differences between groups: mean difference or standardardised mean difference like Cohen’s d

Associations between variables: correlation coefficient (r)

Categorical outcomes: odds ratios or risk ratios

Once you have calculated an effect size, there are then common conventions to interpreting the effect size:

Effect size Small Medium Large
Cohen’s d 0.20 0.50 0.80
r 0.10 0.30 0.50
Odds ratio 1.68 3.47 6.71
Risk ratio 1.22 1.86 3.00

Chen, H., Cohen, P., & Chen, S. (2010). How big is a big odds ratio? Interpreting the magnitudes of odds ratios in epidemiological studies. Communications in Statistics—simulation and Computation®, 39(4), 860-864.

Sullivan, G. M., & Feinn, R. (2012). Using effect size—or why the P value is not enough. Journal of graduate medical education, 4(3), 279-282

6.2.2 Minimally clinically important differences (MCID)

Another way of assessing clinical significance is by using pre-established thresholds of minimally clinically important differences on commonly used outcome measures. For example, the PHQ-9 has a MCID of 3.7 points (Bauer-Staeb et al., 2021) and the GAD-7 has a MCID of 4 points (Toussaint et al., 2020). When comparing participants before and after the study, you could report the average change in your primary outcome measure and compare this to the MCID and you could also calculate what percentage of individual participants’ scores changed more than the MCID.

Bauer-Staeb, C., et al. (2021). Effective dose 50 method as the minimal clinically important difference: Evidence from depression trials. Journal of Clinical Epidemiology.

Toussaint, A., et al. (2020). Sensitivity to change and minimal clinically important difference of the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD-7). Journal of Affective Disorders.

6.2.3 Proportion of participants reaching clinically meaningful improvement

Finally, you may choose to assess clinical significance by reporting what percentage of participants reached meaningful improvement. You can do this using MCID as noted above. If you are using an outcome measure with pre-established cut-offs, you may choose to report how many participants changed from having ‘moderate’ to ‘mild’ symptoms on the measure you have used.

For example, the CORE-10 categorises psychological distress as 0-10 = healthy 11-15 = mild distress 16-20 = moderate distress 21-25 = moderately severe distress 26-40 = severe distress

Then after an intervention using the CORE-10 as an outcome measure, you might want to report what proportion of individual participants decreased from severe to moderate distress or from moderate distress to healthy.
